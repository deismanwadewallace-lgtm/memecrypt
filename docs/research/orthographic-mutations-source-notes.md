# Orthographic Mutations — source notes

These notes support the provisional `OM-##` collection. They establish public
lineage and explain evidentiary limits; they are not image-clearance records.
No linked source image is copied into the gallery.

## OM-01 — Punctuation Saves Lives

[Open English at SLCC’s “Punctuation Memes and Choice”](https://pressbooks.pub/openenglishatslcc/chapter/punctuation-memes-and-choice/)
documents the familiar “Let’s eat, Grandma” form in an educational discussion
of punctuation memes. It is strong evidence for circulation and classroom use,
not for a single point of origin. Grade B.

## OM-02 — Oxford Comma Wars

[Know Your Meme’s Oxford Comma entry](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/oxford-comma)
documents the convention’s migration into recurring online jokes and visual
arguments. The editorial convention is far older than the meme cluster; the
entry supports circulation, not invention of the comma. Grade B.

## OM-03 — A Lot / Alot

[Know Your Meme’s “The Alot” entry](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/the-alot)
connects the form to Allie Brosh’s 2010 *Hyperbole and a Half* post. The famous
creature is copyrighted artwork and is deliberately absent. This collection
isolates only the word-boundary mutation. Grade B.

## OM-04 — Muphry’s Law

[Know Your Meme’s Skitt’s Law entry](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/skitts-law)
groups several names for the principle that writing criticism will contain its
own error. It supports the internet lineage but also shows unstable naming and
competing attributions; the label therefore avoids claiming one inventor of the
underlying editorial joke. Grade B.

## OM-05 — Typoglycemia

[Wikipedia’s transposed-letter-effect article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transposed_letter_effect)
distinguishes a real family of reading effects from the viral email’s false or
unsupported “Cambridge University research” attribution. Readability depends on
word length, context, and which letters move; “only first and last letters
matter” is not accepted as a universal rule. Grade A for the corrective account.

## OM-06 — The OUGH Outbreak

[The British Council’s list of confusing English words](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/blog/confusing-english-words-for-ielts-how-to-avoid-mistakes)
provides an institutional language-teaching example of the divergent
pronunciations attached to `-ough`. It supports the linguistic phenomenon; the
gallery’s composition and wording are original. Grade A.

## OM-07 — TEH

[Know Your Meme’s “Teh” entry](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/teh) documents the
transposition’s use as both a common error and a deliberate internet spelling.
Precise first use is not established, so the record describes a period and
function rather than naming an inventor. Grade B.

## OM-08 — The !1 Phenomenon

[Know Your Meme’s “The !1 Phenomenon” entry](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/the-1-phenomenon)
describes the Shift-key slip and its deliberate elaborations. The physical
keyboard explanation is plausible and widely recognized; a precise single
origin is not claimed. Grade B.

## OM-09 — I Accidentally the Whole Thing

[Know Your Meme’s “I Accidentally” entry](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/i-accidentally)
documents the missing-verb construction’s 2008-era circulation and subsequent
snowclones. The gallery creates a new example rather than repeating a source
image caption. Grade B.

## OM-10 — LOLspeak

[Know Your Meme’s LOLcats entry](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/lolcats) supports
the mid-2000s history of captioned-cat culture and its productive language.
Rights are fragmented across many photographs and creators; the existing M28
register record is D3 / unavailable. This exhibit diagrams grammar and displays
no cat. Grade B.

## OM-11 — Dogespeak

[Wikipedia’s Doge article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doge_%28meme%29)
describes the short, multicoloured phrases and deliberately unusual modifier
combinations that formed the meme’s voice. The source photograph is already
registered as M01 and declined for display. This exhibit presents only an
original typographic analysis. Grade A.

## OM-12 — Alternating-Capital Sarcasm

[Know Your Meme’s Mocking SpongeBob entry](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/mocking-spongebob)
documents the 2017 template associated with irregular capitalization and a
mocking tone. The convention has since become intelligible without the image;
the gallery demonstrates that escaped convention and does not reproduce or
imitate SpongeBob character art. Grade B.

## Writers at Work annex

[Writers Write’s general writing-meme index](https://www.writerswrite.com/writingmemes/)
and [writer’s-block collection](https://www.writerswrite.com/writingmemes/writers-block/)
show the recurrence of deadline and blank-page motifs. [Writing Forward’s “Kill
Your Darlings” discussion](https://www.writingforward.com/storytelling/storytelling-kill-your-darlings)
supports the editing maxim as a writing-culture motif. The annex’s five lines
are newly written for Memeography and do not reproduce these sites’ meme images.

## Deliberate exclusions

- **Grammar Nazi** is historically established internet slang, but the label
  trivializes Nazism and the Holocaust. It may be discussed critically in
  future research; it is not adopted as the museum’s comic voice.
- **The Alot creature, LOLcat photographs, Kabosu/Doge imagery, and Mocking
  SpongeBob** are named only where necessary to understand lineage. They are
  not display assets.
- **The viral typoglycemia paragraph** is not copied. Its false scholarly
  attribution is part of the history under examination.
